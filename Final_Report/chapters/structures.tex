\chapter{Structures}

\section{Responsibilities}

The structures subsystem is the mechanical backbone of Lakitu: it holds every
other subsystem -- the Coral payload, the battery pack, the EPS board, the
OBC, the LoRa radio and its antenna, the GPS module, and the sensor breakouts
-- in a defined position inside a 1U envelope, and keeps them there through
ground handling, launch, the stratospheric flight, and a parachute landing.
Concretely, the subsystem owns five things:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Envelope compliance.} The complete assembly, including every
        protruding fastener, must fit the gondola's merged 1U payload slot,
        where one unit is a 100\,mm cube in the CubeSat
        sense~\cite{cds,kessler}.
  \item \textbf{Placement.} The structure has to realise placement
        constraints set by other subsystems: the Coral's camera must look
        straight down through an unobstructed aperture, the transmitting
        LoRa antenna must sit as far from the GPS receiver as the stack
        allows, and the high-dissipation components stay clustered to
        support the passive thermal concept of the Machine-Learning Payload
        chapter.
  \item \textbf{Retention.} Every component must stay seated under vibration
        and landing shock, without its wiring being pulled loose.
  \item \textbf{Serviceability.} The platform was assembled, modified, and
        re-assembled continuously between bench sessions (EPS bring-up on
        2026-07-07, the endurance run on 2026-07-20), so the structure must
        come apart and go back together quickly without damage.
  \item \textbf{Mass.} The structure's own mass must remain a small fraction
        of the 650\,g flyable-mass ceiling (CR-001).
\end{itemize}

\section{Interfaces}

The structure touches every subsystem, so its interfaces are mostly
mechanical hole patterns sized to the boards they carry:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{ML payload.} The Coral Dev Board Micro
        ($65.0 \times 30.0 \times 6.8$\,mm, 10.4\,g~\cite{coralds}) mounts on
        the dedicated bottom plate, camera facing down through the plate's
        central aperture (Section~\ref{sec:bottomplate}).
  \item \textbf{EPS.} The pack of two Panasonic NCR18650B cells
        (18.5\,mm diameter $\times$ 65.3\,mm, $\approx$48\,g per
        cell~\cite{ncr}) sits in its own tray on an upper level; at roughly
        95\,g it is the heaviest single mass on the stack and drives the
        retention concept (Section~\ref{sec:retention}). The hand-wired EPS
        perfboard mounts on standoffs.
  \item \textbf{OBC / CDH.} The Nucleo board and the sensor breakouts mount
        on standoffs of one uniform height; a separate tray was designed for
        the SD logger.
  \item \textbf{TT\&C.} The LoRa module mounts via its own bracket; the
        antenna is carried by a ring seat in the topmost plate (visible at
        the top of Figure~\ref{fig:v1assembly}), the point of maximum
        separation from the GPS module lower in the stack.
  \item \textbf{Gondola.} The assembled cube slides into the gondola's
        payload frame, which merges two adjacent 0.5U bays into one 1U
        slot~\cite{kessler}; a window in the gondola's underside preserves
        the Coral's optical path (Section~\ref{sec:gondola}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Design Decisions}

Two structural iterations were built. Both share the same architecture: a
stacked-plate layout in the CubeSat tradition -- horizontal
$100 \times 100$\,mm plates carried by four corner columns, one subsystem
level per plate -- manufactured by fused-deposition 3D printing. FDM was
chosen for the same reasons the gondola platform itself is
printed~\cite{kessler}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the highly component-specific geometry (pockets, apertures, cable
        cutouts) would be slow and expensive to machine, but is free in a
        printed part;
  \item a printed plate can be re-designed and re-printed between two lab
        sessions, which the project schedule ultimately depended on;
  \item printing keeps the structure in the same manufacturing ecosystem as
        the gondola frame it integrates into.
\end{itemize}

All plates are printed flat, so the dominant in-plane loads run along the
deposited layers rather than across them. Printed polymers are strongly
anisotropic -- for printed PLA, ultimate tensile strength roughly doubles
when loaded in-plane rather than across layers~\cite{yao} -- and the
manufacturer's data for both materials used here shows the same trend
(Table~\ref{tab:materials}). What changed between the iterations is the
material -- and with it, nearly everything about how well the architecture
worked.

\subsection{First Iteration: A Compliant TPU Structure}

The first structure (Figure~\ref{fig:v1assembly}) was printed in Bambu TPU
95A HF, a Shore 95A thermoplastic polyurethane. The reasoning was driven by
the landing case: a balloon payload ends its flight with a parachute landing
on unprepared terrain, and the first verification milestone on the horizon
was a drop test. TPU promised three things:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Toughness without brittleness.} The datasheet lists an
        elongation at break above 650\,\% and a Charpy impact strength of
        123.2\,kJ/m$^2$ in-plane (86.3\,kJ/m$^2$ across layers) at
        27.3\,MPa tensile strength~\cite{tpu}. A structure printed from it
        cannot crack on impact in any realistic scenario; it deforms and
        returns.
  \item \textbf{Built-in vibration isolation.} An elastomeric frame acts as
        its own isolator between the gondola and the electronics,
        attenuating what reaches solder joints and connectors.
  \item \textbf{Cold tolerance.} The manufacturer credits the material with
        ``impact resistance, flexibility, and cold-temperature
        resilience''~\cite{tpu} -- relevant on a platform that spends its
        cruise phase in stratospheric cold.
\end{itemize}

The retention concept followed the same philosophy
(Figure~\ref{fig:v1brackets}): instead of seating boards on standoffs, each
component received an individually modelled bracket -- a conformal pocket
sized to that component's outline -- so that the part is held over its whole
footprint, cannot rattle, and is mechanically isolated from its neighbours.
The layout made three placement calls:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Coral and battery low.} The two heaviest items shared the
        lowest plate, keeping the centre of mass low, with the camera
        looking down through a central aperture in the base.
  \item \textbf{Antenna on top.} The antenna got its own plate at the very
        top of the stack, carried by the top-plate ring seat.
  \item \textbf{Maximum antenna--GPS separation.} The stack height between
        the two is the point: GPS L1 arrives at a minimum specified
        received power of only $-128.5$\,dBm~\cite{isgps}, while the LoRa
        transmitter may radiate up to $+14$\,dBm~e.r.p.\ in the EU
        868\,MHz band~\cite{etsi} -- a disparity of more than 140\,dB.
        Even out-of-band energy from a transmitter that close can
        desensitise a GNSS front end, which is why receiver vendors warn
        against placing GNSS antennas near active
        transmitters~\cite{ublox}; separation along the stack was the one
        mitigation available for free.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Failure Review of the First Iteration}

Assembled, the TPU structure failed at its most basic job: holding its own
shape. The cause is visible in one line of the datasheet -- TPU 95A HF has
a Young's modulus of $9.8 \pm 0.7$\,MPa~\cite{tpu}, roughly 145 times lower
than the PETG used later ($1420 \pm 160$\,MPa~\cite{petg}). To place both
numbers (Figure~\ref{fig:modulus}): 9.8\,MPa sits at the bottom edge of the
0.01--0.1\,GPa range quoted for rubber at small strains~\cite{etb} -- in
stiffness terms, the plates were sheets of soft rubber -- whereas the PETG
value is ordinary engineering-plastic territory. Since a plate's bending
stiffness scales linearly with the modulus, a TPU plate of identical
geometry carries about two orders of magnitude less flexural rigidity. The
moment components were bolted down, the fastening preload alone was enough
to bow the plates: they would not pull flat, would not stay parallel, and
the stack never closed square under its own assembly forces. Three further
problems compounded this:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Solder joints were not budgeted.} The through-hole joints
        protruding beneath the hand-soldered perfboards and breakouts had no
        clearance in the bracket pockets, so boards rocked on their joints
        instead of seating flat -- a mechanical and an electrical risk at
        once.
  \item \textbf{No wiring volume.} The design reserved no space for the
        harness. Cables had to be forced between plates and around brackets,
        and the assembly degenerated into exactly the kind of undefined,
        strain-loaded mess the brackets were meant to prevent.
  \item \textbf{Brackets did not fit their components.} The conformal
        pockets were modelled from nominal component outlines, but several
        as-built parts -- with headers, connectors, and solder differing
        from the datasheet drawing -- had different dimensions than
        expected, so the very feature meant to improve retention prevented
        installation.
\end{itemize}

The iteration was abandoned rather than patched, and its lessons carried
directly into the second design:

\begin{itemize}
  \item stiffness is the primary structural requirement -- impact tolerance
        must come from somewhere else (Section~\ref{sec:gondola});
  \item retention features must be modelled from measured, as-built
        component dimensions, including solder and connectors;
  \item cable routing is a design input, not an afterthought.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Second Iteration: Translucent PETG}

The second structure keeps the stacked-plate architecture and re-executes it
in Bambu PETG Translucent. The material swap buys back the stiffness the
first iteration lacked -- a Young's modulus of $1420 \pm 160$\,MPa and a
bending modulus of $1610 \pm 130$\,MPa, roughly half the stiffness of the
PLA the gondola frame is printed from (Figure~\ref{fig:modulus}) and
comfortably sufficient for plates of this span -- while remaining moderately
ductile (elongation at break $8.2 \pm 1.3$\,\%, unnotched Charpy impact
strength $37.4 \pm 3.3$\,kJ/m$^2$ in-plane) rather than glassy and brittle;
the datasheet additionally credits the material with resistance to moisture,
UV, and temperature changes~\cite{petg}, none of which hurts on a platform
that ends its mission outdoors in an uncontrolled spot. The translucency is
not cosmetic: with the stack assembled, the interior harness, the seating of
every board, and the battery retention can be inspected visually between
tests without opening the structure. Table~\ref{tab:materials} contrasts
the two structural materials.

The redesign also reversed the first iteration's retention concept. The
brackets are gone; in their place:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Uniform-height standoffs.} Every soldered board sits on
        standoffs of one common height, which provides defined clearance
        for the solder joints beneath each board (the first iteration's
        unbudgeted millimetres), seats every component flat regardless of
        its underside, and standardises fastener lengths across the stack.
  \item \textbf{As-built hole patterns.} Instead of conformal pockets, each
        plate carries custom mounting holes matched to the measured hole
        pattern of its specific component.
  \item \textbf{Cable-management cutouts.} Dedicated cutouts route the
        harness between levels along defined paths -- the wiring volume the
        first iteration never had.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Envelope Recovery and the Dedicated Coral Bottom Plate}
\label{sec:bottomplate}

The first PETG build failed the envelope requirement: with screw heads and
nuts protruding from the outer faces, the assembly stood 2\,mm proud of the
100\,mm slot dimension. The violation was resolved by redesigning the bottom
of the stack. A new bottom plate (Figure~\ref{fig:v2bottom}) hosts only the
Coral: its footprint is inset to $95 \times 95$\,mm so that all external
fastener hardware stays inside the 100\,mm envelope, and its central aperture
frames the camera. The battery pack, which had shared the lowest level with
the Coral since the first iteration, moved onto an upper level. Splitting
the two also decouples the optical interface -- which wants a clean, rigid,
minimal bottom plate directly above the gondola window -- from the heaviest
and most service-intensive component, which wants access for cable ties and
frequent removal. (Dimensions quoted in the figure captions of this chapter
are overall bounding dimensions measured from the project CAD models.)

\subsection{Fasteners and Retention}
\label{sec:retention}

The stack is assembled exclusively with M2.5 nylon machine screws and nuts.
Nylon was chosen over steel for three reasons:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Electrical safety.} Polyamide fasteners are standard PCB
        mounting hardware precisely because a dropped or protruding
        fastener cannot short exposed traces -- and here the screws pass
        directly beside hand-soldered perfboard undersides.
  \item \textbf{RF neutrality.} Nylon cannot detune the antennas the way
        loose metal hardware near a radiating element can, which matters on
        a structure whose top plate carries the LoRa antenna.
  \item \textbf{Mass.} At a density of $\approx$1.14\,g/cm$^3$ against
        $\approx$7.9\,g/cm$^3$ for steel, the full fastener set becomes
        mass-wise negligible.
\end{itemize}

The strength penalty is acceptable by a wide margin: published test data
for molded nylon 6/6 fasteners puts the ultimate tensile load of an M2.5
screw at roughly 160\,N and nut pull-through at
$\approx$135\,N~\cite{microplastics}. For scale -- as an illustrative
figure, not a verified load case -- even if the entire 650\,g flyable mass
(CR-001) decelerated at 20\,g, the resulting $\approx$128\,N would be
shared across several fasteners in parallel, each loaded well below its
single-fastener ultimate load.

The battery pack is the one component where bracket geometry and friction
were judged insufficient on their own: following the 2026-07-20 endurance
session, cable ties are being reinstated around the pack and its bracket --
a retention path that does not depend on the bracket's fit -- so that the
pack demonstrably stays seated under the vibration environment before the
flight and drop campaigns.

\section{Gondola Integration}
\label{sec:gondola}

Lakitu flies inside the reusable gondola platform developed for the
course~\cite{kessler}: a 3D-printed frame enclosed in a styrofoam insulation
shroud, whose payload frame merges two adjacent 0.5U bays into the single 1U
slot the cube occupies. Integration adds two structure-relevant features:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Optical path.} The gondola provides a window at its
        underside, aligned beneath the payload bay, so the Coral's camera
        keeps an unobstructed view of the ground through the bottom-plate
        aperture.
  \item \textbf{Outboard impact protection.} The impact-tolerance role that
        the TPU iteration tried to build into the primary structure moves
        outboard: the styrofoam shroud already absorbs part of the landing
        energy for the components it encloses~\cite{kessler}, and for the
        drop test the gondola will additionally be padded externally with
        sponge material.
\end{itemize}

This division of labour -- a stiff, dimensionally stable payload structure
inside a compliant, energy-absorbing vehicle -- is the direct architectural
conclusion of the first iteration's failure. The gondola platform's own
9\,m drop test also informs the integration: the structural damage it
recorded was concentrated where unsecured test masses struck the payload
frame on impact~\cite{kessler}, which is precisely the failure mode a
rigidly mounted, fully retained payload avoids.

\section{Verification Status}

Table~\ref{tab:structverif} summarises the verification state of the
structural design at the time of writing. The assembled second-iteration
structure carried the full avionics stack through the four-hour
vacuum-and-freezer endurance run on 2026-07-20; the anomaly that ended the
run was an avionics hang, with battery, thermal state, and RF link nominal
at cutoff and no mechanical failure implicated~\cite{anomaly} -- every
board, connector, and harness remained seated through pump-down, cold soak,
and recovery handling. The drop test, in the sponge-padded gondola
configuration described above, is scheduled after this report's deadline;
its procedure is defined, but its results and any resulting design changes
cannot be documented here.
