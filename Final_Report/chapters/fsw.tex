\section{Development Workflow}

The firmware was developed collaboratively on GitHub: each feature or
subsystem was built on its own branch and integrated into \texttt{main}
only after review, with an AI coding assistant (Claude Code) used to help
review and merge branches so that \texttt{main} stayed buildable
throughout. The source tree supports two independent build systems reading
the same code: PlatformIO, the primary command-line build used for
day-to-day development and verification, and STM32CubeIDE/CubeMX
(Eclipse-based), which regenerates HAL peripheral-initialisation code from
a shared \texttt{.ioc} file -- keeping the two toolchains' view of the
hand-added peripherals in sync was itself a recurring integration task.

\section{Mission Phases}

The mission is divided into six flight phases, each representing a physically distinct regime of the stratospheric balloon flight.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lp{10cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Phase} & \textbf{Physical description} \\
\midrule
Standby & System powered on, sitting on ground. All sensors initialising. Ground pressure baseline (P\_ground) recorded here as reference for all altitude calculations. \\
Launch & Balloon released. Sharp upward jerk, brief chaotic motion lasting seconds. Transition from ground-constrained to free flight. \\
Ascent & Balloon stabilised, climbing at 3-6 m/s steadily. Longest active phase, typically 1.5-2 hours. Temperature drops significantly with altitude. \\
Cruise & Balloon fully expanded at float altitude (~25-35 km). Vertical velocity near zero, altitude oscillating within a bounded range. Duration 30 min to several hours. \\
Descent & Balloon envelope bursts. Rapid fall begins, IMU sees sudden shift from near 0g float to ~1g. Parachute deploys, stabilising descent rate. \\
Landing & System touches down. All motion stops. Terminal state, no further FSM transitions. Logging and telemetry continue for recovery; shutdown timer starts. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Mission phases and physical description}
\label{tab:fsw-phases}
\end{table}

\section{Flight State Machine}
\label{sec:fsw-fsm}

The flight software's core is a single explicit state machine: one
authoritative \texttt{FlightPhase\_t} value, advanced by at most one
transition per cycle of the datapool CDH publishes. The loop currently
runs CDH and FSW at 10~Hz (100~ms, \texttt{LOOP\_CDH\_FSW\_PERIOD\_MS} in
\href{\repourl/blob/main/firmware-stm32/Core/Src/main.c}{\texttt{main.c}}). The six phases and the
physical regime each represents were introduced in
Table~\ref{tab:fsw-phases}; Figure~\ref{fig:fsw-fsm} gives the complete
transition graph between them.

\subsection{Redundant, Debounced Transitions}

Every rule in Figure~\ref{fig:fsw-fsm} is built from the same three
ingredients: a primary physical cue, an independent fallback cue from a
\emph{different} sensor, and a persistence window that the combined
condition must hold continuously before the phase actually advances
(Table~\ref{tab:fsw-transitions}). The two cues are combined with OR, not
AND, deliberately: the goal is that one degraded sensor should not by
itself stall a transition the balloon has physically already made. Launch
detection, for instance, fires on a sharp IMU acceleration spike \emph{or}
a barometric altitude rise -- either alone is enough, so a GPS-denied,
IMU-flatlined, or barometer-saturated edge case still has a second way in.
The persistence window itself is an elapsed-time debounce rather than a
sample-count debounce: the main superloop free-runs at whatever rate the
current workload allows, so counting consecutive cycles would make the
debounce duration depend on CPU load, which counting elapsed milliseconds
does not.

\centering

\toprule
\textbf{From} & \textbf{To} & \textbf{Primary cue} & \textbf{Fallback cue} & \textbf{Window} \\
\midrule
Standby & Launch & IMU accel $>$1.3\,g & Baro altitude rise $>$3\,m & 1\,s \\
Launch & Ascent & Baro altitude $>$100\,m & GPS descent-rate $>$5\,m/s & 3\,s \\
Ascent & Cruise & GPS descent-rate $<$0.5\,m/s & Baro altitude band $<$10\,m & 30\,s \\

\subsection{Sensor Admissibility}
\label{sec:fsw-admissibility}

The FSM does not decide for itself whether a sensor reading is trustworthy
-- that judgment is made once, centrally, and reused everywhere. A single
admissibility check gates every cue in Table~\ref{tab:fsw-transitions}
before it is allowed to steer a transition: a sensor is usable only if CDH
currently marks its datapool group valid \emph{and} FDIR has not flagged
that equipment faulted \emph{and} staged reduced mode has not disabled it
(Section~\ref{sec:fsw-reduced-mode}). This is the same three-source
gate -- data validity, fault state, policy state -- applied uniformly to
GPS, IMU, and barometer alike, so no transition rule special-cases a
degraded sensor; it simply stops seeing that cue at all. The corollary is
an accepted limitation rather than a hidden one: if \emph{both} backing
sensors for a transition are inadmissible at the same time, the phase
cannot advance on that rule and the vehicle holds its last known-good
phase until a sensor recovers, rather than guessing.

\section{Fault Detection, Isolation, and Recovery (FDIR)}
\label{sec:fsw-fdir}

FDIR is the flight software's single point of fault detection, but it is
deliberately not a point of hardware access. Every monitor in
\href{\repourl/blob/main/firmware-stm32/docs/FMECA.md}{\texttt{docs/FMECA.md}} follows the same handling-level model -- contain a
fault at the lowest level that can absorb it (driver retry, single-device
reinit, bus restart, processor reset, system-wide degradation), escalating
only when the lower level keeps failing -- and every one of those monitors
is implemented as one row in a table-driven update loop rather than a
family of bespoke fault handlers. What FDIR does when a fault trips is
always the same three things: raise a fault flag, set a bit in a recovery
request mask, and stop -- the actual recovery action belongs entirely to
whichever subsystem owns the hardware in question (Figure~\ref{fig:fsw-fdir-loop}).
The handling levels themselves form a fixed escalation ladder
(Table~\ref{tab:fsw-fdir-levels}): a fault is always handled at the lowest
level that can contain it, and only climbs the ladder once the level below
has kept failing.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{p{0.9cm}p{3.0cm}p{6.4cm}p{2.8cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Level} & \textbf{Scope} & \textbf{Mechanism} & \textbf{Owner} \\
\midrule
L0 & Inside a driver & Retries, CRC/plausibility checks, bounded HAL timeouts & Subsystem owner \\
L1 & Single equipment & Debounced fault flag + periodic protocol reinit request & FDIR detects, owner executes \\
L2 & Bus / subsystem group & I2C bus restart, SD remount & FDIR requests, CDH/owner executes \\
L3 & Processor & IWDG reset, SCV persistence across reset & FDIR + hardware \\
L4 & System & Staged reduced mode: disable one subsystem update per further reset & FDIR policy, FSW executes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\end{table}

The execute/acknowledge half of the loop lives entirely on the owning
subsystem's side -- here, CDH consuming a single-device reinit request at
the top of its own update, before that tick's sensor reads:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C]
/* Execute + acknowledge -- CDH_Update(), before this tick's sensor reads. */
uint16_t device_requests = FDIR_GetReinitRequests();
if (device_requests & EQUIPMENT_IMU) {
    CDH_FDIR_RecoverIMU(&hi2c1);              /* the actual hardware recovery */
    FDIR_AcknowledgeReinit(EQUIPMENT_IMU);    /* tell FDIR the bit was serviced */
}
\end{lstlisting}

\texttt{CDH\_Update} does not know why the bit was set, only that it was --
the detect/request side that set it runs independently, in FDIR's own
module, and neither side calls into the other's code.

This separation keeps the fault logic itself hardware-agnostic and lets
one mechanism cover six unrelated failure modes (Table~\ref{tab:fsw-fdir-instances}):
FDIR's job stops at recognising that GPS, IMU, and barometer faults are
all ``sensor on the shared I2C bus went bad,'' while CDH's job is knowing
that the concrete recovery for one sensor is a protocol reinit and the
concrete recovery for two-or-more-at-once is a full bus restart with a
bit-banged clock-clear pulse -- a distinction FDIR does not need to
understand to detect the fault correctly.

\toprule
\textbf{Fault} & \textbf{Executor} & \textbf{Recovery action} \\
\midrule
GPS / IMU / baro timeout or implausible reading & CDH & per-device protocol reinit, rate-limited \\

\end{tabular}

\label{tab:fsw-fdir-instances}
\end{table}

Every reinit request is also rate-limited, and the rate is itself policy,
not a fixed constant: most equipment gets a 10\,s cooldown between requests,
but GPS gets a 60\,s cooldown of its own -- re-initialising the module more
often than that disturbs fix acquisition rather than helping it, so most
GPS outages are better left to resolve environmentally (FMECA C1) than
retried aggressively. LoRa runs a dual-threshold version of the same idea,
with its own consecutive-failure and failure-rate windows tuned
independently of the sensor monitors; that policy is detailed in the TT\&C
chapter (Section~\ref{sec:ttc-fdir-radio}) rather than repeated here, since
TT\&C is both the executor and the one place the counters it feeds are
actually visible in telemetry. FDIR also holds a policy layer above any
individual monitor: the ground/bench-settable manual-disable mask described
in Section~\ref{sec:fsw-persistence}, which turns a subsystem off
independently of, and on top of, whatever staged reduced mode already
decided.

\section{System-Level Recovery: Watchdog and Reduced Mode}
\label{sec:fsw-reduced-mode}

Below the per-equipment monitors sits a processor-level backstop for the
failure mode none of them can see: initialization or the superloop itself
hanging (FMECA F1). An independent hardware watchdog (IWDG, nominal
10\,s timeout, FR-011) is armed immediately after HAL initialization and
refreshed during startup and once per superloop iteration -- but that
kick is conditional, not unconditional. Kicking the watchdog
unconditionally would defeat its own purpose for exactly the failure mode
it exists to catch: a logic hang that keeps looping without doing useful
work would still execute the kick line and never reset. FDIR instead
exposes a single ``healthy enough to kick'' verdict, and the kick is only
issued when that verdict holds, so a system that is technically still
executing instructions but has stopped making progress (a stale datapool
that outlives a bus-restart attempt, for example) is allowed to reset
rather than be kept artificially alive.

A single reset is recoverable by design: the phase, the equipment fault
state, and the reset count all live in the persistent SCV
(Section~\ref{sec:fsw-persistence}), so a reset resumes the mission rather
than restarting it from Standby. Repeated resets are a different problem
-- a persistent fault re-triggering every boot, without ever escalating
past what a single watchdog cycle handles. FMECA F2 addresses this with a
staged reduced mode: once the persisted reset count exceeds a threshold,
each further reset disables one more subsystem's update call, in a fixed
order, rather than either ignoring the pattern or giving up entirely
(Table~\ref{tab:fsw-reduced-mode}). There is no power-switching hardware on
the platform to isolate a suspect subsystem outright -- the same
constraint the EPS chapter notes for battery over-discharge -- so peeling
subsystems off one at a time, cheapest first, is the only isolation
available. FDIR itself and the watchdog kick are exempt from every stage:
whatever else is disabled, the system must still be able to detect its own
health and still be able to reset.

\centering
\begin{tabular}{p{2.3cm}p{3.2cm}p{8.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Reset count} & \textbf{Disables} & \textbf{Rationale} \\
\midrule
$<$3 & nothing & a single reset is the expected, fully-recoverable case \\
3 & SD logging & cheapest to lose; a hung SD write is a plausible repeat offender and logging is not safety-critical \\
4 & CDH sensor acquisition & removes the most complex, bus-driving subsystem next \\
5 & TT\&C & telemetry stops; the vehicle is now flying blind to the ground but still flying the FSM \\

\section{State Persistence Across Resets}
\label{sec:fsw-persistence}

FSW and FDIR own the one structure that has to survive a reset: the
35-byte SCV (spacecraft configuration vector) -- flight phase, per-equipment
fault and enable bitmasks, the watchdog reset count, and the boot count and
last reset reason. Ownership includes the flash mechanics themselves:
unlike the datapool backup described in the CDH chapter, which is a
separate mirror CDH maintains independently, the SCV's load/backup/erase
logic lives in FDIR's own module
(\href{\repourl/blob/main/firmware-stm32/Core/Src/fdir/scv.c}{\texttt{fdir/scv.c}}) and is called
directly from \texttt{FDIR\_Init()} and the superloop, with no CDH
involvement. \texttt{FDIR\_Init()} restores the newest valid record (a
16-bit magic word and CRC-16 guard against a blank or corrupt page) before
anything else runs, so every other subsystem's own \texttt{\_Init()} sees
a consistent post-reset state from the first tick. The full field-by-field
layout is given in Appendix~\ref{app:fsw-fdir} (Table~\ref{tab:fsw-scv-fields}).

Two of the fields are worth calling out specifically. First,
\texttt{watchdog\_reset\_count} only increments when the classified reset
reason is actually the watchdog -- any other reset reason (power-on, pin,
software) proves the system is not stuck in a boot loop and re-arms every
reduced-mode stage from Table~\ref{tab:fsw-reduced-mode}, so a clean power
cycle is never mistaken for the eleventh watchdog reset. Second,
\texttt{equipment\_manual\_disable} is a ground/bench-settable mask, held
independently of the staged reduced-mode logic: where
\texttt{equipment\_enabled} is rebuilt from \texttt{EQUIPMENT\_ALL\_NOMINAL}
on every boot (so a normal reset re-arms everything reduced mode turned
off), the manual-disable mask persists as-is and is re-applied on top of
that rebuild, so a subsystem someone has deliberately disabled on the bench
stays off across resets rather than silently coming back. As of this
report no command interface writes this field yet -- it is a policy hook
FDIR already honours, wired up on the detection side, still waiting on an
uplink or debug-console path to set it.

Splitting ownership this way -- FSW/FDIR decide \emph{what} needs to
survive a reset and own the flash write, CDH's separate backup region
guards the datapool history -- keeps the two persistence paths from racing
each other, and means the 1\,s persistence budget in FR-010 is met by the
same write that already happens once per cycle rather than a special-cased
one-off save on every transition.
